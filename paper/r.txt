sdos: stochastic spectral densities of very large sparse Hermitian and generalized
eigenproblems
by kernel polynomial and maximum-entropy methods, with spectral windows,
projections, vibrations and their validation to 106 atoms
David A. Drabold1
1

Department of Physics and Astronomy, Ohio University, Athens, Ohio 45701, USA
(Dated: November 1, 2026)

We describe sdos, an open Python package that computes the density of states (DOS) and related
spectral quantities of very large sparse Hermitian matrices A, and of generalized problems Ax = λBx
with B Hermitian positive definite, from Chebyshev moments estimated with random vectors. The
spectral density is reconstructed either by the kernel polynomial method (KPM, the default) or
by maximum entropy (Maxent, with a bootstrapped solver); band energies, Fermi levels, local and
projected densities of states, harmonic thermodynamics and exact HOMO–LUMO gaps come with
the same interface, and eigenpairs inside a spectral window (with inverse participation ratios) are
available by shift-invert or Chebyshev filtering. The package treats real and complex Hermitian
operators, explicit sparse matrices and matrix-free operators, and electronic (orthogonal and nonorthogonal) and vibrational problems. We validate every ingredient against dense diagonalization or
against independent codes: Kwon and Xu tight-binding models, DFTB Hamiltonians and overlaps
against DFTB+ (matrix elements agree to 8 × 10−7 eV), SIESTA matrices, finite-difference Hessians
from LAMMPS against its dynamical_matrix for the Stillinger–Weber and ACE potentials. On a
workstation (WSL2, 19 GB) a density of states of a 884 736-atom Kwon silicon model (3.5 million
orbitals) takes about 70 s, a non-orthogonal DFTB problem with 442 368 orbitals about 380 s, and a
matrix-free 2 097 152-atom crystalline Si spectrum (8.4 million orbitals, 170 moments) about 32 min
in 1.8 GB; the cost is linear in the number of orbitals (measured exponent 1.01). We report where
the methods do not scale (shift-invert, incomplete Cholesky setup, exact moments of high order,
Maxent with many exact moments), what is new relative to earlier work (non-orthogonal KPM
by preconditioned conjugate gradients was introduced by Röder, Silver and Kress), and practical
recommendations: KPM by default, Maxent when high-order moments are expensive, and exact
low-order moments for integrated quantities.
I. INTRODUCTION

The density of states (DOS) ρ(E) = N −1 Tr δ(E−H) of
a large Hamiltonian H determines band energies, Fermi
levels, thermodynamics and, through its local variants,
much of the interpretation of electronic and vibrational
structure in disordered and nanostructured materials.
For matrices of dimension N ∼ 105 –107 , as arise for
amorphous networks, nanostructures and large supercells, diagonalization (O(N 3 ) time, O(N 2 ) memory) is
out of reach, whereas the moments µn = N −1 Tr Tn (H̃)
of ρ with respect to Chebyshev polynomials of the scaled
operator can be had in O(N ) operations per moment
from matrix–vector products with a few random vectors, because the trace can be replaced by a stochastic estimator [1, 2]. Two routes lead from moments to
a density: the kernel polynomial method (KPM) [3–5],
a damped Chebyshev series that is linear, positive with
the Jackson kernel [6] and easy to use, and the principle of maximum entropy [7–9], which finds the positive density with maximal entropy that reproduces the
moments and which, for the same moments, resolves
sharp features better. Both belong to the family of
linear-scaling electronic-structure methods reviewed by
Goedecker [10]; related moment methods are the recursion method [11], the plane-wave moments method [12]
and Fermi-operator expansions [13], and modern surveys

of stochastic spectral-density estimation are in Ref. [14].
Electronic problems in a non-orthogonal basis and vibrational problems with unequal masses are generalized
eigenproblems, Ax = λBx. Röder, Silver and Kress
[15] showed that KPM extends to this case by applying
B −1 A with a preconditioned conjugate-gradient (PCG)
solve of B for every Chebyshev step, and demonstrated
it for a titanate/platinum interface and amorphous diamond. sdos implements this idea and adds alternatives
for applying B −1 (block-Jacobi, incomplete Cholesky, algebraic multigrid, and a fixed Chebyshev polynomial that
makes the whole recursion an exact polynomial map), a
Maxent reconstruction, exact low-order moments, spectral windows, projections and a set of interfaces to tightbinding and force-field codes. Open general-purpose
tight-binding codes for large systems, such as KITE [16],
Kwant [17] and TBPLaS [18], are built around orthogonal models: we found no mention of an overlap matrix in
their papers (we did not audit their source code), and we
make no claim beyond that. Our contribution is therefore
an open, tested implementation and a quantitative validation, not a new method; we state in each section what
is established elsewhere and what we measured here.
The outline of the paper is as follows. Section II
gives the methods and the design choices; Section III
the validation against exact results; Section IV three applications that correspond to published calculations (a

Exact low-order moments and impartial vectors

Drabold and Sankey [9] introduced impartial vectors,
chosen to weigh all eigenvectors equally. Their construction minimizes the penalty P (x) = (⟨x|x⟩ − 1)2 +
(⟨x|H|x⟩ − µ1 )2 + (⟨x|H 2 |x⟩ − µ2 )2 by conjugate gradients, with the exactly known µ1 = N −1 TrH and
µ2 = N −1 TrH 2 (both O(N ) from the matrix), so that
the first three moments of x are exact; we reproduce their
narrowing of the band-energy distribution. Only three
moments are constrained: constraining more makes the
ensemble biased, as we verified by the rms z-score of the
moment residuals (1.8, 4.6, 4.5 for K = 4, 8, 16 constrained moments, against ≈ 1 for an unbiased ensemble). The unbiased way to exploit exact low moments
is substitution: µn for n ≤ K can be computed exactly
in O(N ) because [Tn (H̃)]ii depends only on a neighbourhood of radius ≈ n/2 hops of site i, so the first K moments of every sample are replaced by the exact ones
(exact_moments=K, default K = 4 when cheap). The
cost per site grows polynomially with K, not with N . Table I measures all options on one problem: at equal cost
every variance-reduction option beats plain Rademacher
vectors by a factor of order 20–50, with K = 8 best.

Bootstrapped Maxent. Following Drabold’s practice
of climbing in the number of moments from the band
edges found with fewer, maxent_bootstrap solves for
M = 16, 32, 64, . . . in turn, starts each stage from the
previous multipliers, and after each stage restricts the
integration support of the dual to the interval where the
density exceeds 10−4 of its maximum, padded by two resolution lengths π/M . No states are allowed outside the
edges, so the multipliers need not build huge exponentials to suppress the density in the empty margin of the
window. The support is a modelling statement, checked
by the residual of the moments; a stage that fails to converge is widened and retried, and if it still fails the last
converged stage is returned (with converged=False and
the number of moments used). For graphene with exact
moments, M = 400 converged in 9 s with the bootstrap
against 28 s without (and without convergence), at the
same error; the support barely shrinks for graphene because the band fills its window, so for that case the gain
is due to the warm starts.

Vibrations

For a force-constant matrix Φ and masses Mi , D =
M −1/2 ΦM −1/2 has eigenvalues ω 2 . vdos works with

G.

Interfaces and implementation

sdos is a pure-Python package (NumPy [23], SciPy
[24]; Python 3.14 in the present tests); everything else
is optional. Tight-binding models: the orthogonal Kwon
silicon model [25] (constants checked against Table I and
Eqs. (4) and (8) of the paper) with Bloch twists (complex Hermitian matrices), a chunked assembler for 106
atoms and a matrix-free periodic stencil for crystals; and
the Xu–Wang–Chan–Ho carbon model [26] (parameters
and the cubic tail checked against the paper). DFTB
Slater–Koster (.skf) files, read from a user-supplied directory (the pbc-0-3 set [27] is distributed under CC
BY-SA 4.0 and requires citation of Refs. [28, 29]; it is not
bundled), non-self-consistent [30] (zeroth-order Hamiltonian H0 and overlap S). SIESTA H and S through
sisl [31–33], and LAMMPS force fields [34] through the
matrix-free Hessian. A command-line tool (sdos) covers the common cases. The test suite has 178 tests (run
with OpenMP/BLAS capped at 4 threads), including the
comparisons with exact diagonalization listed in Sec. III,
a regression test against the original single-file code and
tests that the documented examples run.

The DFTB reader. Reading the DFTB format correctly required three conventions that the available documentation does not spell out, which we recovered by
comparing whole matrices with DFTB+ [35] (Sec. III D):
a table announced as ngrid rows contains ngrid − 1,
row j sits at r = j ∆r (r = 0 is the omitted row) and
the first 19 rows of the silicon file are placeholders for
r < 0.4 Bohr; the p orbitals are ordered (py , pz , px ); and
each integral is continued past the end of its table by a
quintic that reaches zero, with vanishing first and second
derivatives, one Bohr later and matches value, slope and
curvature of the table’s last row. The non-orthogonal
pair convention follows from the file name: in A-B.skf
the first orbital of each integral sits on A, so a (p on A,
s on B) block uses the B–A file.

III.

VALIDATION

All tests compare with exact diagonalization or with
an independent code; the benchmarks use DTW4096,
a 4096-atom model of amorphous silicon from the
Djordjevic–Thorpe–Wooten family [36] (relaxed with
ACE, cell 43.6 × 43.7 × 43.9 Å3 ), at Γ unless stated,
since the cells are large and the k-point dependence of
an amorphous Si cell this size is weak.

A.

Unit tests

The directive for the package fixed the following tests,
all of which pass: (1) a cubic Anderson model; (2) the
same with Peierls phases (complex Hermitian); (3) a 216atom Kwon silicon model; (4) a random sparse A with
a sparse positive definite B whose condition number is
tuned from ≈ 10 to ≈ 104 , real and complex; (5) a Keating dynamical matrix with unequal masses in both forms
(mass-weighted, and generalized with M ); (6) both B −1
modes give the same moments; (7) regression against the
original code. Beyond these: Maxent robustness (large
windows, default models), the Mulliken sum rules, local exact moments against the dense matrix, the inertia
counts, and the interfaces (Secs. III C–III E).

C.

Complex Hermitian problems

A Bloch twist k makes the Hamiltonian of a periodic
supercell complex Hermitian, Hij = h(dij )eik·dij , and its
spectrum is the union of the primitive-cell bands at the
k points folded onto it. For a 216-atom Kwon supercell
and for the DFTB model we verify that the generalized
spectrum of the twisted supercell equals this union to
10−9 eV and that H is Hermitian to 10−12 ; the same
builder is used in chunked form for 106 atoms and agrees
with the unchunked one exactly. The matrix-free stencil of a 163 -cell supercell (131 072 orbitals) at a generic
twist has a Hermiticity defect of 1.8×10−17 and its KPM
and Maxent densities (M = 170, R = 6) agree with the
exact k-sum with L1 distances 0.036 and 0.047 against
the 0.1 eV-broadened reference. For DTW4096 with the
DFTB model the density at a k point of the cell’s own
reciprocal lattice, a genuinely complex generalized problem, looks like the Γ one (L1 to the Γ spectrum 0.021
against 0.024 for Γ itself, Table III); a dense complex
generalized diagonalization of 16 384 orbitals does not fit
in this machine, so there is no exact reference at that k
point and we say so.

D.

Non-orthogonal problems: DFTB and SIESTA

Against DFTB+. DFTB+ [35] can write the realspace H0 and S (WriteRealHS). For a randomly displaced 64-atom silicon cell of 10.9 Å (where pairs interact through two periodic images) the matrices built
by sdos from the same .skf files agree with DFTB+
to 7.9 × 10−7 eV in H (largest element 10.8 eV) and
4.3 × 10−8 in S, and the generalized eigenvalues to
3 × 10−6 eV. A distorted Si–H cluster (which tests the
heteronuclear sign convention and the use of the reversedpair file) agrees to 7.3 × 10−7 eV and 5.5 × 10−8 . The
residual is the difference between our cubic spline and
DFTB+’s polynomial interpolation; in the interior of the
table the two agree to 10−10 .
DTW4096. Table III gives the DFTB problem of

SIESTA. The SIESTA Harris/single-zeta matrices of

Fullerenes and graphene

Drabold, Ordejón, Dong and Martin [41] followed
the icosahedral fullerenes C60n2 from C60 to C3840
with moment-Maxent densities of states (“of order 150
moments”, no artificial broadening), a shifted-Lanczos
HOMO–LUMO gap and local densities of states, and
observed that the DOS of C2160 and C3840 approaches
graphene; the geometries were relaxed with an order-N
tight-binding scheme [42]. We repeat the calculation with
the Xu–Wang–Chan–Ho Hamiltonian [26] on geometries
relaxed with the LCBOP potential [43] (which reproduces the strain energies and radii of Ref. [42] to within a
few percent, while REBO and AIREBO overestimate the
strain by 35–70%), generated with a Caspar–Klug (Goldberg) construction for any (m, k). Fig. 4a shows C3840
from M = 150 moments: Maxent follows the exact spectrum (here a 0.1 eV Gaussian-broadened histogram of
the 15 360 exact eigenvalues, computed in 124 s), including the van Hove peaks near +9 and +12 eV above EF ,
whereas KPM at 150 moments (resolution ≈ 0.45 eV)
is visibly broader; this is the regime in which the 1995
calculation was done, and the sharp graphene-like fea-

tures it showed are reproduced. Our model is not theirs,
so we compare shapes, not numbers: our exact HOMO–
LUMO gap of C3840 is 0.284 eV against 0.10 eV in their
Fig. 3 (and 1.537 against 1.62 eV for C60 ); we did not
establish whether the difference is due to the Hamiltonian or the relaxation (the ideal sphere gives a degenerate HOMO/LUMO and a wrong spectrum, so it is not
informative).
The exact gaps (Table V) follow gap ∝ N −0.47 , which
is the scaling of a confined massless Dirac fermion, ~vF /R
with R ∝ N 1/2 . From the graphene bands of the
same Hamiltonian we find a true Dirac point (gap at
K < 10−14 eV) with ~vF = 4.20 eV Å (6.4 × 105 m/s),
and gap · Req /~vF rises with N to 1.92 at C6000 (Fig. 4c),

VI.

RECOMMENDATIONS

1. KPM is the default: linear, positive (Jackson),
never fails, resolution πa/M ; for the maximum resolution of a large system increase M (a few thousand is cheap; cost is linear in M ). Beyond the
mean level spacing or the noise floor, more moments only sharpen sampling speckle.
2. Maxent when high-order moments are expensive
or noisy (M ≲ 300), when positivity and sharp
edges matter, and for integrated quantities (band
energy, gap region); it extracted 6–10× lower errors
from identical moments here. Use the bootstrapped
solver; expect it to stop at a few hundred exact
moments of singular densities.
3. Random vectors: the default exact-moment substitution (K = 4) is almost always worth it; K = 8
for band energies; impartial vectors constrain only
three moments and are nearly as eﬀicient at lower
cost per vector (Table I).
4. Generalized problems: PCG with block-Jacobi
is the default; the Chebyshev polynomial gives an
exact polynomial map when the conditioning is

known; IC(0) lowers the iteration count if its setup
is cheap.
5. Report the sampling error of every integrated
quantity, and overlay KPM and Maxent: where
they agree, the feature is in the moments.
VII.

CONCLUSIONS AND AVAILABILITY

sdos computes densities of states and related spectral
quantities of very large sparse Hermitian and generalized
problems with a documented, tested, open implementation. Its ingredients are established (stochastic traces,
KPM, Maxent, non-orthogonal KPM by PCG); what we
add is the combination, the options for the overlap inverse, bootstrapped Maxent, windows, projections and
interfaces, and a quantitative validation against exact diagonalization and independent codes at sizes from 103 to
8 × 106 orbitals. We found linear scaling in all momentbased computations, three conventions of the DFTB file
format that are not in the format description, and prior
art that we credit. The limits are stated above.
